Nine tiles are enough to show what makes some proofs written by artificial intelligence so hard to read. Suppose I want to know the width of a floor and all I have been told is that it contains nine identical square tiles. In reply, someone draws a square of the same area on a separate sheet and calls the number of tiles along one of its sides the measure. Three times three is nine, so the measure is three. If I am then told that my floor is three tiles wide, I would like to know who ruled out a corridor nine tiles long and one tile wide, for which both the drawing and the calculation would be exactly the same.

The machine introduces a concept because it has seen it alongside the ones under discussion in other arguments on the subject. It defines the concept precisely and proceeds as though naming it had established that it applies here. Nine and the square are so familiar in combination that the reader almost supplies the connection on the author's behalf. To deduce the width of the floor, I need to know how the tiles are laid. Without that information, I can follow the arithmetic to the last digit and still be unable to verify the conclusion.

How many primes are there up to one hundred?

A similar question occurred to me when I read the announcement of a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. To see what was being claimed, take 11, which is exactly divisible only by one and by itself. Integers greater than one with this property are called primes. If we went up one step each time we met a prime, between ten and twenty we would rise four times, at 11, 13, 17 and 19.

There are twenty-five primes up to a hundred. If we wanted to know how many we would find up to a billion, it would help to have a curve that followed the staircase closely. The curve below passes just above twenty-nine at a hundred. How far out could its estimate be as we carry the count further?

To study this discrepancy in 1859, Riemann used a rule whose connection with the primes was already known to Euler. A function is a rule that assigns a result to each admissible value. Riemann's choice, the zeta function, is defined at first by a sum to which every positive integer contributes. Since every integer greater than one can be broken down into primes in only one way, apart from the order of the factors, Euler had been able to write the same sum as a product with one factor for each prime.

Riemann also studied zeta for numbers that take two measurements to place on a sheet of paper, one horizontal and one vertical. Points at which the calculation gives zero are called zeros of the function. From the locations of those in the strip between zero and one he derived corrections to the estimate of the prime count. The first five we meet as we go up all lie on the vertical line in the middle. The Riemann hypothesis says that all the zeros in the strip lie on that line, however high we go. The bound on the error we set out to measure depends on that word ‘all’, which no finite drawing can give us.

Alpöge's four pages

Now that we have met the primes and the zeta function, we can turn to the announcement of 20 November 2026. Levent Alpöge released the proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he reports finding with Claude. In a four-page note he explains that studying a sum required a chain of operations. How much longer should the chain become as the number of terms increased? With sixteen terms we could arrange sixteen squares into a square and count four along each edge. Four, which gives sixteen when multiplied by itself, is its square root. Alpöge credits Claude with suggesting that the length of the chain should grow as the square root of the number of terms.

The proof containing that choice runs to about seventeen million lines in Lean, a language in which a computer can check each step. On 21 November the Lean community and the community behind its mathematical library, Mathlib, confirmed that the conclusion matches Riemann's statement and that several independent programs verified the steps. Alpöge, who helped make the discovery, writes that he wants to understand the argument more fully so that he can explain it.

Alessio Figalli has described the difficulty of reading the proof on fluid motion that OpenAI published on 8 September. Produced by one of the company's systems, it concerns the Navier–Stokes equations, which describe a fluid whose layers encounter resistance as they slide past one another. In the case studied, the fluid starts at rest and, under a smoothly varying force, can reach arbitrarily high speeds as it approaches a finite time. In the proof's 166 pages, Figalli observes, some definitions appear so abruptly that even a mathematician struggles to see where they are needed.

The recipient's calculation

Anyone who knows that 84 is the product of 2, 2, 3 and 7 can tell without dividing that 6 divides 84, since the list contains the 2 and 3 we need, and that dividing by 5 leaves a remainder, since 5 is missing. The prime factorisation already contains all the divisors of a number. Knowing it gives an advantage that has found a use in private correspondence, provided the primes are large enough that someone who knows only their product has difficulty recovering them.

To choose the two primes, it helps to know how many numbers we can expect to have to test. From the average rate at which our staircase rises, given by the prime number theorem, we can estimate that about one odd number in 115 is prime near a hundred digits, and one in 345 near three hundred digits. We can therefore draw odd numbers of the desired size at random and test them one by one for primality, with some idea of how long the search will take on average.

In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman were working on a problem in cryptography, the art of keeping communications secure. How do you send a private letter to somebody with whom you have never had a chance to agree on a secret? They proposed that the recipient publish the instructions for transforming the letter before sending and keep to themselves the instructions that would let them read it on arrival. The first correspond to the public key, the second to the private key. The following year at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman put the idea into practice by choosing two primes and making their product public. The method appeared in a journal in 1978 and took its name, RSA, from the initials of their surnames.

The three authors saw that the pair of keys could also be used for signing. With a private key you can put a digital signature on a document, which anyone with the document and the corresponding public key can verify. When an Android phone receives an app update, RSA is one method it can use to verify the signature and the integrity of the contents. When a secure connection is first opened, a website can also use an RSA signature to prove to a browser that it holds the private key associated with its certificate, the electronic document that binds the public key to the site's name.

In his book on quantum computing, David Mermin reduces RSA to a calculation we can do by hand with the primes 3 and 11. The recipient publishes their product, 33, and another number, which is 3 in this example and tells the sender how many copies of the message to multiply together. To send 2, the sender calculates 2 × 2 × 2, divides the result by 33 and sends what is left over, the remainder. Since 33 goes into 8 zero times, the number sent is 8. Transforming the message before sending is called encryption, and recovering it at the other end is decryption.

Having received 8, let us take one copy of it, then two, then three. Multiplying two copies gives 64, which leaves 31 after we subtract 33. To add the third copy, we need only multiply 31 by 8. The 33 we discarded would have become eight whole groups of 33, leaving the same remainder. A product of equal factors is called a power. In 8³, the repeated number 8 is the base, and the little 3, the exponent, counts the copies.

If we start at 1, multiply by 8 at each step and keep the remainder after division by 33, we get back to 1 at the tenth step. The whole cycle must then begin again, because whenever the same remainder appears, the same rule is applied to it. The number of steps in the first complete cycle is called the period. On the way, at the seventh step, we also meet the 2 that the sender started with. How can the recipient, who has received only 8, know to stop there?

Because the recipient chose the two primes, they can subtract one from each and multiply the results, giving (3 − 1) × (11 − 1) = 20. That is how many integers from 1 to 33 remain once the multiples of 3 and 11 have been crossed out. Euler's theorem applies to each of the twenty remaining numbers. Multiplying twenty copies of any of them gives a remainder of 1 on division by 33. The number 8, which already completed its cycle on the tenth step, has gone round twice by the twentieth.

Suppose the message is one of these twenty numbers. The recipient knows that each copy of the encrypted number contains three factors equal to the message, as the published rule specifies. To recover the message, we need twenty factors whose product leaves a remainder of 1, and one more factor to multiply by that 1. The factors come in groups of three, and it takes seven groups to reach twenty-one.

3 × 7 = 20 + 1

The recipient can choose that 7 before the letter arrives. It is the private exponent, usually written d. In our example, seven copies of the received 8 contain twenty-one factors of 2 and leave a remainder of 2. The same key also works for messages divisible by 3 or 11, as one can check by working out the remainders with respect to each of the two primes.

How long is the cycle?

With 33 the factors are apparent at a glance, and ten steps suffice to go round the whole cycle of remainders for 8. Let us replace 33 with the product of 47 and 59, namely 2773, and look at the stretch from step forty to step one hundred. At step forty the remainder is 1019. Multiplying 1019 by 8 gives 8152, and subtracting two groups of 2773 leaves 2606. We can place each point exactly from the one before it, although the heights of those points give little clue to the length of the cycle.

To measure the period, we compare two copies of the sequence, with one shifted by a given number of steps. For each pair we go right by the first remainder and up by the second. If the remainders agree, the point lies on the diagonal. After a shift of 1333 steps, none of the plotted pairs lies there. At 1334 steps they all do, because it is on step 1334 that the initial remainder, 1, first returns.

If the primes had hundreds of digits, the recipient could still use the factors to derive an exponent at which the remainder returns to 1, and construct the private key without knowing the length of the shortest cycle. Someone who has only the public information can calculate the remainder of a power quickly even when the exponent is enormous. Finding the period in general on a classical computer has no comparably efficient known method. The abundance of primes lets the person making the key pick two large ones, and the difficulty of recovering that particular pair from their public product helps keep the secret.

Why the square root appears

Let us return to Riemann's line. To bound the error in counting the primes to the precision in question, we need to determine where every zero in the strip lies. We know that if a zero fell to one side of the middle line, another would lie at the same height and distance on the other side. Folding the paper along the line would bring the two points together. This symmetry means that the crease passes halfway between the two points.

To see what the property gives us, mark two points at the same height, one a quarter of the way across the strip and the other three-quarters of the way across. Call the point halfway between them the midpoint of the pair. The midpoint lies on Riemann's line, even though both points lie off it. If, having proved where the midpoint lies, I claimed to have proved that the zeros lie there, I would have changed the subject of the sentence. To pass from the midpoint to the zeros, I would still have to prove that the distance between them is zero.

A machine that has so often encountered the word centre next to symmetry can move from one to the other by association, with the confidence of someone drawing a conclusion. The centre really does exist, exactly where the machine puts it. I say the definition appeared at random because when I go back to the assumptions about the zeros, I cannot find one that entitles me to infer their positions from the position of the midpoint. Someone who knew nothing about zeta could have done the very same calculation.

In two lines it is easy to retrace your steps. Suppose I read ‘as shown above’ ten pages later and several other definitions have meanwhile been built upon that first one. I have to work back through them to find out which objects the conclusion still applies to. Every calculation along the way could be right. For someone trying to verify that they form part of the proof at hand, the difficulty is finding the line at which the argument began to establish a different property.

I would ask Alpöge to let me shorten Claude's chain on paper. In his note he says that each link had to reproduce an effect that was obtained in a related problem by counting repetitions. I would like to see the line at which the estimate he needs to control the sum fails if I take some links out. Even if it took me months to follow that calculation, by the end I would be able to say why the square root appears precisely there. As long as I have to invent the reasoning that justifies its use, the page remains unreadable to me even when I can repeat it in full.
